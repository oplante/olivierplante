# Comparison

Opened on 3 October 2026. Structural lessons only. Visual systems were not copied.

## Emil Kowalski, emilkowal.ski

What it does well: the present job is two sentences in the first person. Projects and writing are named lists, not pitches. You know who he is before you scroll.

Lesson taken: open with a plain statement of the work, then a record you can scan. The narrative stays short. The jobs carry the detail.

Rejected: a project index and a writing feed. Olivier's CV is a career, not a component library. Also rejected the "today" liveness. Nothing in the CV updates like a status.

## Brian Lovin, brianlovin.com

What it does well: one sentence for role, city, and employer. The rest of the home page is an index of his own work. The about page holds the longer bio, so the first screen stays quiet.

Lesson taken: role, place, and employer belong in the first line. Depth can follow on the same page because this site has no essays to link.

Rejected: the section zoo (apps, music, Hacker News, social rows). Three-column project grids. Anything that turns a bio into a directory.

## Lee Robinson, leerob.com

What it does well: a first-person paragraph with concrete employers and years, then a human line (family, music) that is still specific. Themes are a list of things he has actually written about.

Lesson taken: prose can hold a career if every sentence has a fact. One human detail from the CV (cooking, baking, growing food) belongs on the page so it does not read as a LinkedIn export.

Rejected: belief lists, topic taxonomies, and newsletter energy. There is no essay archive in the CV, so inventing one would be slop.

## Rauno Freiberg, rauno.me

What it does well: a single precise line (who, from where, working with whom) does the job of a headline. Projects sit below as names, not feature marketing.

Lesson taken: the hero is one idea, not a stack of value props. No feature columns under the name.

Rejected: manifesto fragments ("Make it fast. Make it beautiful.") and a craft-log frame. Those fit an interaction designer showing process. They would be costume on a product manager's career page.

## Paco Coursey, paco.me

What it does well: present tense, then past tense, then a now note, then a plain "reach me" line. The page sounds like a person who builds things.

Lesson taken: contact is an address you can copy, not a form and not a row of identical social cards. A now-adjacent personal line is enough.

Rejected: tool showcases, music sub-pages, and slogan footers. Also rejected dark, monospace, "magical details" styling. Calm paper, not a portfolio theme.

## What the page takes from all five

First person. One present-tense line in the hero. A scannable record of jobs. One human sentence from the CV. Contact as email and LinkedIn only.

What it refuses from the genre: equal feature cards, a centered dark hero, icon rows, fake metrics, stock photos, testimonial grids, and a blog that does not exist.
